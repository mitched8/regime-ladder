How to read the ladder

What the number is, what it isn't, and the one thing that decides whether the project works. Every picture on this page is from synthetic data with known answers; real ones replace them at Gates 2 and 3.

1The number

For each component the book is built from — the ATM straddle, the 25-delta risk reversal, the 25-delta fly, each with its hedge rule — the ladder says what one fresh unit of it, put on today, earned over the next 1, 3, 5, 10 and 20 days in history, on days that looked like today. "Looked like today" means the regime label on the entry date: carry, transition or crisis, from a transparent score on the vol surface.

Read it as: the marginal expected earn of carrying one unit of this component for the next h days from today's state. Cash per unit of standard notional. Not a trade signal; a number to tilt composition with.
EV(h | entry regime)  =  average over past entries in that regime of  [ cumulative net P&L to day h ]
                         held h days, no exit rule, hedged as the backtester hedges, per unit notional

2Why fresh entries at fixed horizons

The obvious alternative — estimate what a component earns per day in each regime and add the days up — fails for options because a one-month structure at day 2 and at day 18 are different exposures. Averaging across ages mixes them, and the mix itself depends on the regime. Measuring the cumulative P&L of fresh entries at each horizon sidesteps the whole problem:

  1. Every observation starts from the same reference exposure and ages through the backtester's own repricing. The exposure is right by construction.
  2. Regime changes that happen inside the window are already in the measured P&L. Nothing is added for them, so nothing can be double-counted.
  3. The distribution — tails, probability of profit — is measured, not simulated.

  1. 1Horizon. Trading days from entry. The last row is to expiry. Short rows are the actionable ones for a book that is re-cut continuously.
  2. 2EV. Mean cumulative P&L of past fresh entries in today's regime, in cash per unit notional. Shrunk slightly toward the all-regimes mean when evidence is thin.
  3. 3Interval. Where the true mean plausibly is, allowing for the fact that daily entries overlap: twenty-day outcomes from consecutive days share nineteen days of market. The interval widens with horizon because the evidence genuinely thins — see 5.
  4. 4ES 5%. Average of the worst 5% of outcomes at that horizon. Unshrunk. Tail statistics are never pulled toward the centre.
  5. 5n_eff and episodes. Effective number of independent observations after overlap, and the number of separate stretches of this regime in the history. Few episodes means the number rests on a handful of events, whatever n_eff says. Under ten is said in words on the card.
  6. 6Regime dependence. The same horizon's outcomes split by whether the entry regime held or broke during the window, with how often it broke. It explains the headline; it is not a forecast. A component that is helped by a break (insurance) and one that is hurt by it look different here and the same in the EV column.
  7. 7Where it earns. The expected earn per day in the age bucket with the largest effect, against day one. This is how the ladder applies to inventory you already hold — see 4.

4The ladder as a picture, and the increments

5Why to trust the interval

Before any real data was touched, the whole pipeline was run on synthetic data where the right answer is known exactly: a three-state regime path, trades whose earn depends on regime and on remaining tenor, a daily shock shared by every live trade, and noise. With the true labels, the ladder's 90% intervals contained the true answer 89% of the time across six independent histories. That is the evidence the statistics are implemented correctly. It says nothing about real markets, and is not meant to.

6The crux: the labeller

Everything above conditions on a label that has to be produced in real time from the surface. The labeller is deliberately simple — a composite stress score, smoothed, with two boundaries and a hysteresis band to stop flicker — and simplicity has a price: it lags. Every regime onset is seen a few days late, so the first days of each new regime carry the old label.

7What this is not

  1. Not a trade signal. It is a conditional description of what a component has earned, with its uncertainty. Composition decisions remain the trader's.
  2. Not a forecast of regime change. The "regime dependence" split uses what actually happened; a forward-looking version (today's catalysts, positioning, dealer gamma) is a later phase and is kept out until the plain version has proven itself.
  3. Not a model of existing positions. The ladder is for a fresh unit; the increments approximate aged ones. Repricing an actual aged position along simulated paths is the Phase 4 extension.
  4. Not production. About 1,200 lines of research code. If it earns its keep, it is rebuilt properly.

8How it gets to a decision

GateQuestionSynthetic, true labelsSynthetic, estimated labels
1 · IntegrityDoes the data reconcile, and do hand-verified golden trades reproduce?passpass
2 · SeparationDo entry labels separate outcomes at h ≤ 10, with stable sign?passpass
3 · Go / no-goOut of sample, does the ladder beat both the unconditional statistic and a direct model on the same features?passfail

Thresholds are fixed in advance and are not loosened to make a gate pass. If the ladder beats the unconditional statistic but not the direct continuous model, regimes become the language the card speaks while the numbers come from the continuous model — an acceptable outcome. If it beats neither, the unconditional multi-horizon ladder with honest intervals still ships; it is a better description of what the components earn than the status quo.
